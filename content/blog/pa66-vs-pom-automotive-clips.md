**Short answer:** choose **PA66** (usually 30% glass-filled) when the clip sees heat above about 100 °C, automotive fluids such as brake fluid or glycol coolant, or repeated flexing. Choose **POM** when the clip holds a steady load for years, slides against another part, or must keep tight dimensions in a humid environment.

Both are the go-to engineering plastics for clips, fasteners and retainers, and both mould well in multi-cavity tools. The difference is in *how* each one fails — so the right choice depends on what your clip actually has to survive.

## Side-by-side comparison

| Property | PA66 (unfilled) | POM (acetal) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Melting point | about 265 °C | about 175 °C |
| Continuous service temperature | about 130–150 °C | about 90–100 °C |
| Moisture uptake at saturation | about 8.5% | about 0.8% |
| Size change at saturation | about 1.3–1.8% | about 0.2% |
| Creep under sustained load | Higher | Lower (better) |
| Fatigue under repeated flexing | Better | Lower |
| Sliding wear and friction | Good | Excellent |
| Brake fluid, glycol coolants | Good | Poor (swells) |

*Typical values for general-purpose grades; always check the datasheet of the exact grade.* Values from the Nylon vs POM comparison guide listed under Sources.

## When PA66 is the right choice

**Heat.** POM's melting point of about 175 °C limits it to roughly 100 °C in continuous use. PA66 melts around 265 °C, and glass-filled PA66-GF30 keeps its stiffness much closer to engine-bay temperatures. For clips on wire harnesses near the engine, under-hood brackets or anything close to the exhaust, PA66-GF30 is the default.

**Automotive fluids.** POM swells in brake fluid and glycol-based coolants, while PA66 resists them. If the clip can be splashed with these fluids, choose nylon.

**Repeated flexing.** Snap-fit clips that are pushed in and pulled out many times — trim panels removed for service, for example — benefit from nylon's better fatigue resistance.

**Cost.** PA66 is generally less expensive than POM, which matters at clip volumes of lakhs of pieces a month.

## When POM is the right choice

**Constant load.** A spring clip or retainer that stays loaded for the life of the vehicle slowly relaxes through creep. POM resists creep better than nylon, so it holds its clamping force longer.

**Humidity and tight tolerances.** Nylon absorbs water and grows — PA66 can change size by over 1% at saturation, while POM moves only about 0.2%. For clips that must hold a precise dimension in a humid climate, POM is the safer choice.

**Sliding and wear.** POM has very low friction and wear against steel, which is why it is used for bushings, sliders and rotating parts. A clip that slides on a rod or rail should usually be POM.

## The nylon trap: brittle clips on day one

PA66 is dried before moulding and leaves the machine almost completely dry. Dry nylon is at its stiffest and most brittle, and it toughens as it takes up moisture from the air. A clip that cracks during assembly on day one may be perfectly fine a week later.

That is why nylon clips are often **conditioned** — exposed to controlled moisture after moulding — before they are packed. If you are seeing assembly breakage on new nylon clips, ask your moulder about conditioning before changing the design.

## How the two materials mould differently

These differences matter for cost, tooling and quality:

- **Drying.** PA66 must be dried before moulding. Material from open bags with moisture above 0.2% needs thorough drying, otherwise you get splay marks and weaker parts. POM normally needs no drying if stored dry.
- **Melt temperature.** PA66 runs hot: around 260–290 °C, and roughly 280–300 °C for glass-filled grades. POM runs much cooler: about 180–230 °C for homopolymer and 190–210 °C for copolymer. Overheating POM releases formaldehyde, so temperature and residence time must be controlled.
- **Mould temperature.** Glass-filled PA66 typically needs a mould at about 80–90 °C for good crystallinity and stable dimensions.
- **Shrinkage.** The two shrink differently, so a mould built for one material usually cannot run the other without dimensional changes.

## A quick decision checklist

1. Will the clip see more than about 100 °C? → **PA66-GF30**
2. Will it touch brake fluid or coolant? → **PA66**
3. Is it flexed in and out repeatedly? → **PA66**
4. Does it hold a constant load for years? → **POM**
5. Does it slide, rotate or rub? → **POM**
6. Must it hold tight tolerances in humid conditions? → **POM**

If your answers point both ways, the usual compromise is a tougher or lubricated grade of one material — your moulder can suggest grades to trial.
